For most of my career, there has been a clear point where my ability to design something ran ahead of my ability to build it. I'm quite technical for a designer and I can code, but there has always been a practical limit to how far I could take an idea before the effort involved in implementing it stopped being worthwhile.

That limit is much further away now.

## My coding journey

I started with HTML, CSS and JavaScript, then PHP (thanks to WordPress), some Python and Ruby, and more recently React and Next.js. I've generally been able to build the things I wanted to build, but usually slowly and with a fair amount of trial and error. I could work things out eventually, but there was often a point where I knew what needed to happen without knowing enough to make it happen quickly enough to know whether it was worth the effort.

## The easier implementation becomes, the more important it is to understand what you are asking to be implemented

This is the bit I find most interesting, particularly as a service designer.

There is a slightly lazy argument that technical knowledge matters less now because AI can do more of the technical work for you. My experience is almost the opposite.

The easier implementation becomes, the more important it is to understand what you are asking to be implemented.

That does not mean knowing how to write every line of code yourself. It means understanding enough about technology to know how it can shape a service, where the complexity sits, what data is required, what needs to persist, what can be automated, what should remain a human decision, and what happens when systems have to interact.

Those things have always mattered in service design. They matter even more when it becomes cheap to turn an idea into something that works.

If you treat technology as a black box, AI gives you a faster black box. If you understand the technical layer, it gives you a much wider design space.

## Technical competence makes the service design better

A lot of service design involves looking across people, process, policy, data and technology and working out how those things combine into an actual service. If you only understand the visible interaction, you are missing quite a lot of what determines whether that service works.

Technical competence lets you ask better questions.

You can spot when a process exists only because an old system required it. You can challenge a hand-off that could be automated. You can see when two apparently separate user needs are really symptoms of the same underlying data problem. You can understand whether a constraint is genuinely difficult or simply inherited from the current implementation.

It also helps avoid one of the easiest traps in digital work: taking an existing process and rebuilding it on a nicer screen.

If you understand what technology is capable of, you are much better placed to ask whether the process itself still needs to exist. That might mean removing a step entirely, bringing information forward, combining tasks that were previously separate, automating a decision that does not need human judgement, or changing the order of a workflow because the underlying systems no longer impose the old sequence.

That is service design work, not software development. Technical understanding simply makes it possible to do that work better.

## AI makes that understanding much more usable

Previously, I could identify an interesting technical possibility but still need a developer to help me explore it properly. Sometimes that was absolutely the right thing to do. Sometimes it made a small experiment too expensive to justify.

Now I can often take the idea further myself. I can work with a real API, use realistic data, make something persist, model state changes or test whether two parts of a service really can interact in the way I assumed they could.

That does not mean I should be building production systems on my own. It means I can get further into the problem before implementation becomes the limiting factor.

For a service designer, that is extremely useful.

It also means technical judgement matters more, not less. AI can produce something that runs while still making poor choices underneath, and working software is persuasive. If you do not understand enough to question the result, it is easy to confuse "this works" with "this is a sensible thing to build".

## Maybe the useful question was never "should designers learn to code?"

I have never found "designers should learn to code" particularly useful as a blanket piece of advice. Plenty of excellent designers do not need to code, and being able to write software does not automatically make someone better at designing services.

The more useful thing is to understand technology well enough to design with it rather than around it.

Understand what a database means for the service. Understand what an API allows you to do. Understand permissions, state, automation, data quality and failure. Understand enough to have a meaningful conversation with engineers, and enough to recognise when the technical shape of a solution is creating unnecessary work for users.

AI makes that understanding easier to put into practice because it handles more of the translation between idea and implementation.

That is the part I find exciting.

Not that I can suddenly pretend to be a developer, but that I can carry service design thinking much further into the actual behaviour of a service.

The wall has moved.

And the easier it becomes to build things on the other side of it, the more important it is to understand what is worth building in the first place.
